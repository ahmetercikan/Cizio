## Gönderilen metin

Hello,

We have not resubmitted the app. Here are our answers to all nine questions.

1. Cizio teaches Turkish speaking children between five and nine how to draw, one step at a time, with a voice reading every instruction aloud. The help is withdrawn as the child gets better: first the line is traced, then only dots are shown, then the child draws it alone. After each step the app compares the child's stroke with the target, says which part was missed and never shows a number. There are two ways to draw: on the screen with a finger or a stylus, with Apple Pencil pressure and palm rejection, or on real paper, where the child watches the steps, draws, takes a photo, and the app finds the drawing in the photo, lines the model up over it and marks it. Without this a child needs an adult next to them, or copies a video that never looks at what came out. Around the lessons there is a dress up studio, an English club and a small 3D island.

2. Turkish speaking children from five to nine, and the parents who install it for them. Every instruction is spoken and every button carries a picture, because children this age cannot yet read a long sentence.

3. Step by step drawing apps for children exist and gave us the idea, but the ones we tried are passive: they show a shape to trace and accept whatever the child draws. Cizio marks the stroke itself and says where it went wrong, takes the help away as stars are earned, marks drawings made on paper through the camera, speaks Turkish for children who cannot read, and wraps the lessons in a world that brings a six year old back the next day.

4. Cizio is not new. The Android version has been on Google Play through several releases, 1.4.0 is in production there, and the build in front of you is that product brought to iOS. Two people test it. One of us installs each build on his own iPhone through TestFlight, and his eight year old daughter uses the public web build. She is the age the app is for, so most changes come from watching her. The home screen was cut to four cards because more overwhelmed younger children. The narration was extended after we saw that a child who cannot read stops exactly where the voice stops. The opening was made lighter so the app starts quickly on an older phone.

5. It stands on its own and has its own history on Google Play. Two other apps of ours are on this account: Sayio teaches arithmetic strategy to children from four to fourteen, and Isim Sehir is a word game families play against each other.

6. Cizio is the oldest and largest of the three and the other two came after it, not out of it. Marking strokes, reading a drawing off paper and a 3D island have nothing to do with arithmetic practice or a competitive word game, and its children are the youngest of the three audiences.

7. There is shared code and we would rather set it out plainly. The three apps use the same stylesheet and interface shell, which is why they look related, plus small helpers for sound and for keeping a profile, and the same Capacitor setup. All of that started here, in Cizio. What makes Cizio itself was written for it: the lessons and their artwork, the stroke marking, the fading help, the camera alignment, the island, the rewards and the voice library.

8. No. Apart from React, Vite, Capacitor, three.js and Firebase there is nothing shared. No white label kit, no bought template, no generation service, no licensed content. We wrote the lessons, drew the artwork and wrote the text. The narration was made with a licensed text to speech service from our own scripts, and the sound effects come from a royalty free library.

9. Nobody asked us to build it and nobody supplied the idea, the content or the branding. It was written by Ahmet Mesut Ercikan, a developer on this team, and published under this account, which also holds the copyright. No part of it came from a template or a generation service.

If a video or the Play listing would help, we will send them.
